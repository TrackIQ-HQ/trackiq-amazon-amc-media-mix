# The twelve slides

The deck argues one case: **where the money is going versus where the new
customers are coming from.** Every slide either builds that case or is cut.

Each content slide carries a kicker (fixed, in the template) and a
**takeaway title** — a full sentence stating the finding. Never a topic
label. "DSP performance" is a label; "DSP returns $0.59 on the dollar when
it acts alone" is a title.

| # | Slide | The one job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title | Client, period, three KPIs |
| 2 | Pivotal finding | The single sentence the reader leaves with |
| 3 | Where the money went | Three months of media against revenue |
| 4 | The touch ladder | More touches convert more and return less |
| 5 | Channel combinations | Which mixes recruit and which harvest |
| 6 | The DSP question | What DSP is worth, stated honestly |
| 7 | Cost of a new customer | The ranking the recommendation rests on |
| 8 | Time to conversion | The attribution window and the event lead time |

| 10 | The recommendation | One reallocation, with the arithmetic |
| 11 | Data integrity | What a reader must know before acting |
| 12 | Closing | Three figures |

## Slide 2 — the pivotal finding

Written last, after the analysis. It is the answer to "if the client reads
one slide, which one?" Usually a tension: spend moved one way, an outcome
moved the other, and AMC explains the gap.

Three figures on the dark slide, each over a gold rule. Percentages and
multiples, not long dollar strings.

## Slide 6 — the DSP question, honestly

The hardest slide to write without overclaiming. AMC shows which channels
appeared in a purchase path. It does **not** show which channel caused the
purchase.

So the slide has two callouts and both are mandatory:

- **What this does not say** — co-occurrence is not incrementality; a
  holdout test would be needed to prove causation.
- **What it does say** — the standalone performance and the combination
  performance, which are measured, and what follows from them.

Never write "DSP drove $X". Write "$X of sales came from paths containing a
DSP impression".

## Slide 9 — role, not name

The last column names the role the *numbers* show, against the role the
campaign name claims. A line called "Prospecting" with 1% new-to-brand is a
retention line, and the deck says so. This column is the slide's whole
point; a generic restatement of the ROAS wastes it.

## Slide 10 — the recommendation

One reallocation, netting to zero. Every row carries a confidence chip, and
the two callouts carry the arithmetic and the assumption inside it. See
`assets/derivations.md` for how the payoff is computed.

If the analysis does not support a reallocation, say that on the slide and
give the one thing to measure next instead. An invented recommendation is
worse than no recommendation.

## Slide 11 — never delete this

Four cards plus an overall confidence line. If the pulls were clean, the
cards say what was checked and found sound. Confidence is stated as a
number out of ten, split between direction and magnitude, because those two
are rarely equal.

## Voice

- Metric-first and plain. "Cost per new-to-brand purchase", not "customer
  acquisition efficiency metric".
- Say the consequence, not the observation. Not "DSP ROAS is 0.59x" but
  "DSP returns 59 cents on the dollar when it acts alone".
- Name the caveat in the body at readable size. There are no footnotes in a
  TrackIQ deck — anything not worth a body line is not worth including.
- No emoji. No vendor or tool names other than TrackIQ and Amazon's own
  surfaces.
- Quantify only what the data supports. "$12,970 a month" beats "significant
  savings", and neither beats admitting the number is not there.
